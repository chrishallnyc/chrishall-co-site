# American history layer: editorial notes and sources

Reviewed September 14, 2026. This first collection contains 78 curated places across all 50 states, 9 primary themes, and 6 editorial story sequences. It is a starting collection, not an exhaustive history of a state or the United States. The initial deeper collections are California's Gold Rush, Texas ranching and cattle trails, and New York's immigration and civil-rights stories.

## What was verified

The linked official pages below were consulted through browser search results or opened pages for the historical subject and core narrative. Sources are the National Park Service, state parks, the Texas Historical Commission, and the institutions responsible for the sites. Summaries are original concise paraphrases. Where institutions give different dates or numerical totals, the entries avoid relying on contested precision. For example, the Goodnight entry does not assert a JA Ranch founding year, and the Alamo entry does not give a disputed defender count.

The `landscape` sentences are editorial geographic interpretations connecting the story to visible terrain, waterways, or settlement patterns. They are not quotations. Some follow explicitly stated explanations in the source (for example, Hanford cooling water, Saint Croix's winter isolation, and Angel Island's isolation); others are clearly contextual interpretations. They should be presented as a geography connection, not an attributed historical quotation or a demonstrated single cause.

## Time and location conventions

- `startYear` and `endYear` define an inclusive window for finding a story by era. They are not necessarily the construction or operating dates of a building. The human-readable `period` describes the intended subject.
- Broad century and decade windows are editorial approximations. Exact event years are used for specific well-documented events. Archaeological periods are approximate and must retain their “c.” labels.
- `-15000` is an internal open-past sentinel for undated ancestral traditions, not a claim of occupation in 15000 BCE. Do not display this numeric value as an archaeological date. Use the provided period text. Stories about living traditions can appear in every era.
- An end year of 2026 with a “present” or “living” label means the associated tradition or remembrance continues; it is not an expiration date.
- Filter eras by interval overlap: `place.startYear <= era.end && place.endYear >= era.start`.
- Each pin uses an approximate, manually curated site location or representative point. These are suitable for exploration, not surveying, route finding, access instructions, or locating archaeological remains. No source claims centimeter-level coordinates.
- Current state assignments are browsing categories. They do not imply that modern state boundaries, nations, land ownership, shorelines, or place names existed in the displayed era.
- Ellis Island's pin represents the main immigration building on the original island associated with New York. Much of the expanded island lies in New Jersey; the collection's single NY category is not a boundary assertion for the entire island.
- The Juneteenth pin is the present exhibit at Ashton Villa's carriage house. It does not claim that General Orders No. 3 was first read from Ashton Villa's balcony.
- Story trails are curated reading sequences, not surveyed historical trail geometries, driving routes, or a claim that every stop is chronologically ordered.

## Editorial scope

Each place has one primary theme for a simple first filter. Themes inevitably overlap. A complete expansion should add multiple theme tags, community-authored narratives, archival images with licenses, local sources, and more historical places in every state. Indigenous history is represented both before European colonization and as continuing cultural and political life. Cowboy stories include Mexican ranching knowledge, Mary Ann Goodnight's work, and the displacement connected with expansion, not only a heroic frontier narrative.

No source text, photographs, or archival images have been copied into the app. Source links are embedded in every story card's data. This content does not represent an NPS, state-park, tribal, or museum endorsement of the website.

- [California State Parks: Empire Mine](https://www.parks.ca.gov/?page_id=499) — underground mining and closure in 1956.
- [California State Parks: Malakoff Diggins park brochure](https://www.parks.ca.gov/pages/494/files/MalakoffSHPFinalWebLayout051517.pdf) — the 1850s–1880s hydraulic-mining period.
- [California State Parks: Gold Rush Overview](https://parks.ca.gov/?page_id=1081) — river gravels, Sierra foothill sites, and Sacramento's riverboat commercial role.
- [Texas Historical Commission: Fort Griffin visitor guide](https://www.thc.texas.gov/sites/default/files/2023-12/FortGriffin_VisitorsGuide.pdf) — river crossing, cattle route, and military context.
- [King Ranch timeline](https://king-ranch.com/about-us/history/timeline/) — founding in 1853; the narrative in the app primarily draws on the linked Kineños account.
- [NPS: Pecos Pueblo](https://home.nps.gov/places/the-pecos-pueblo.htm) — approximate 1350–1838 settlement window.
- [NPS: Mesa Verde places](https://www.nps.gov/meve/learn/historyculture/places.htm) — approximately 550–1300 community window and distinct mesa and cliff settlements.
- [NPS: Dred Scott case](https://www.nps.gov/jeff/planyourvisit/dredscott.htm) — the Scotts began their freedom suits in 1846; Supreme Court ruling in 1857.
- [NPS: National Park index, Illinois section](https://www.nps.gov/parkhistory/online_books/nps/index2009_11.pdf) — Lincoln family's residence in Springfield, 1844–1861.

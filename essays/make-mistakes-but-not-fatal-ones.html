MAKE MISTAKES, BUT NOT FATAL ONES

two is one. one is none.
100 in the game · 0 ruined
fig. 3 — one hundred careers, each making random mistakes. the ones that touch zero never return. refresh to run it again.

You can make a thousand mistakes in a career. Shoot a soft take, misjudge a client, buy the wrong gear, say the wrong thing in the wrong room. Every one of them survivable, most of them useful. There is exactly one category you cannot afford: the fatal mistake — the one that takes you out of the game permanently. The distinction sounds obvious. Almost nobody organizes their life around it.

Watch the figure above for a minute. A hundred careers, wandering. The wandering isn't the problem — wandering is where all the compounding happens. The problem is the floor. Touch it once and the line goes quiet forever. It doesn't matter how brilliantly a faded line was performing before it faded. Zero times anything is zero.

In my trade the fatal mistakes have names: the record button never pressed, the interview shot out of focus, the memory card that filled at minute forty of a take that cannot be repeated. Every profession has its own list, and the amateurs and professionals are separated not by talent but by who knows their list cold. You learn the list by experience — ideally someone else's.

Against everything on the list, you engineer. Not hope — engineer. The military owns the phrase: two is one, one is none. One recorder on a shoot is zero recorders, because the universe holds a grudge against single points of failure. Two cameras, two recorders, two of every cable that would end the day. I drive a pickup truck for the storage space, and the storage space is for backups. People call this paranoia. It has never once felt like paranoia on the day the first recorder died.

Then you check. Not twice — the best people in any field are not double-checking. How many times do they test the microphone before the Oscars? Not two. Twenty-five. The technique that actually works: check it, walk away, come back and check it as if you'd never seen it before. Fresh eyes find what familiar eyes have already forgiven. And behind the redundancy sits a deeper layer the military also named — PACE: primary, alternate, contingency, emergency. Four pre-decided answers to "what if this fails." You won't always get four deep. The point is never standing anywhere with only one.

Notice what this buys you, because it's the opposite of what it looks like. It looks like caution. It's a license for aggression. The lines in the figure that survive get to keep taking risks — the survivors compound. Once the fatal category is engineered away, you can swing hard at everything else, make bold mistakes, recover, and swing again, because nothing on the table can kill you. The reckless look brave and exit early. The prepared get to be brave for decades.

I treat every project as a no-fail mission — not because every project deserves it, but because you're only as good as your last one, and reputations die from single bullets. The favor-job you took casually can end you as surely as the flagship. So the checklist runs regardless of the invoice.

Make mistakes. Make plenty — cheap ones, early, recoverable, informative. Just know your fatal list, engineer against every line of it, and stay in the game. The game rewards whoever's still playing.

— chris hall

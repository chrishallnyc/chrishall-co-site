A useful correction

The “forest” was already designed.

Sutro planted eucalyptus, cypress and pine over a landscape of grass, dunes and cultivation.

Newell–Murdoch later cut much of it for streets and lots, preserving conspicuous stands as atmosphere. “Country in the city” was both landscape and sales copy.

The ugly / advertised restrictions18

Newell–Murdoch sales brochure, reported by SF Planning • original historical slur

“No Japanese, Chinese or Negros.”

Another developer broadsheet promised “no Mongols, Africans or ‘shack builders’” and assured “the person of taste and refinement” that nothing “disagreeable” would enter.

This was not a stray prejudice beside the real-estate product. Race, class, housing type, building cost and setbacks were bundled as one guarantee of “exclusiveness.”

Language as reported by SF Planning, Gardens in the City from an undated Newell–Murdoch brochure, and transcribed/recreated by WNP from a separate broadsheet. No recorded deed scan was located. “Negros” is the reported source spelling.

The exclusion machine

Explicitly barred named Asian and Black groups.

A $4,000 minimum house cost filtered buyers by wealth.

No flats, apartments, double houses or more than one house per lot.

The physical result—large detached homes on controlled lots—outlived judicial enforcement of the advertised racial restrictions.

Exclusion → integration20

Law moved faster than custom

Judicially unenforceable in 1948.
Not magically undone.

The Supreme Court decision stopped judicial enforcement; it did not erase deed language or informal gatekeeping. Prominent Black residents—including Willie Mays and Terry Francois—arrived later.

Governance / myth audit26

Myth

“Residents still own and maintain all the streets.”
×

Fact

The city maintains the streets today.

Residents did take over substandard developer-built streets, sewers and lighting in 1918–19. San Francisco later assumed street and curb responsibility.

The association remains consequential. Its published materials document parcel assessments supporting the Maybeck clubhouse, trees/landscaping and events; its guidelines request review of exterior work.

Sources disagree on the handoff date: WNP says 1978; a local history ties a final assessment to 1984. Present-day responsibility is the secure fact.

Sources: WNP; SF Public Works street/sidewalk standards report; Association 2014 assessment newsletter and design guidelines; local history. Assessment spending evidence is historical, not a current-dollar budget.

Biography of a house / 536

The sale history chart

There isn’t one.

No verified public price series was found for 554 Magellan.

The official Recorder resolves the confusion. A 1993 deed of trust secured a $160,000 loan—debt, not price. A 1999 quitclaim deed moved the same people’s interest into their revocable trust “WITHOUT CONSIDERATION,” with $0 transfer tax—not a sale.

No arm’s-length sale appears in the official online index from 1990 onward. Zillow and Redfin estimates are not sales. They stay off the chart.

Assessment is not price: the 2025 secured-roll assessed land + improvements total $90,726. That assessed figure is not a market valuation.

The honest verdict38

The good / the bad / the ugly

All three are visible in the same streets.

The good

A coherent work of landscape urbanism

Rail access, mature canopy, humane curves, architectural texture, quiet streets and a rare neighborhood room by Maybeck.

The bad

Scarcity preserved as character

Low density, an older population, high owner occupancy, high values and weak interior commerce limit who can enter and how the neighborhood adapts.

The ugly

Exclusion was written into the product

Racial restrictions and some residents’ documented fear of formerly homeless neighbors are not footnotes. They are part of how “tranquility” was produced and defended.

Forest Hill deserves admiration precise enough to survive the truth.

Synthesis based on the cited historical, demographic, housing and property records throughout this deck.
